The jam that travels backward

A line of cars moves right. A tiny hesitation can grow into a slow patch that drifts left through the line—so you meet the jam even though no obstacle is sitting there.

Watch the arrows: car motion goes →; the red slow patch tends to go ←. This is a schematic toy, not a calibrated traffic forecast. Try changing the ripple and watch whether the slow patch fades or bunches up.

Two directions, one road

Cars cross through the moving queue, but the queue boundary can travel upstream. In traffic-engineering language, that moving boundary is a shock wave. A real road's outcome depends on demand, capacity, spacing, and driver response; this tiny model only makes the counterintuitive direction visible.

Reading: FHWA, “Shock Waves and the Accordion Effect” describes forming queues as backward-moving shock waves.
